6.1Research Is a Project: The Triple Constraint Meets Uncertainty

A project is a temporary endeavor with a defined beginning, end, and unique deliverable — which describes every funded research undertaking exactly. What makes R&D project management its own discipline is that one corner of the classic management triangle keeps moving.

The triple constraint is project management's first law: scope (what will be delivered), time (by when), and cost (for how much) are coupled — change one and at least one other must move, with quality living in the middle as the casualty of pretending otherwise. A funded project is a promise expressed in all three currencies at once: the proposal's objectives are the scope, its Gantt chart the time, its line-item budget the cost. Reviewers read the three against each other, and the commonest fatal flaw is not a bad idea but an incoherent triangle — eighteen months of scope on a twelve-month schedule, or a budget bearing no arithmetic relation to the activities.

What distinguishes R&D is irreducible uncertainty — the Frascati criterion of Chapter 1 returning as a management problem. A bridge builder knows what the bridge will look like; a researcher, by definition, does not fully know what the experiment will show. Good R&D management therefore does not eliminate uncertainty (that would eliminate the research); it contains it: uncertainty is confined to the scientific outcome, while everything containable — schedule logic, spending discipline, data management, compliance — is managed as tightly as any bridge. The panel's confidence question is precisely this: has this team confined the uncertainty to the science?

Key Concept
The proposal is the triangle: objectives = scope, Gantt = time, budget = cost. Every review question about feasibility is a question about whether your three documents describe the same project.
Astronomy in Focus
Astronomy adds a constraint the triangle doesn't show: the sky's schedule. Observing seasons, lunar phases, monsoon months, and eclipse dates are immovable dependencies — a Philippine observing campaign that ignores the habagat season has planned twelve months of which five may be cloud. Your Gantt chart must be drawn against the almanac, not just the calendar.

6.2Lifecycle and Governance: Gates, Deliverables, Milestones

Projects live in phases, and funded projects add a governance skeleton: someone approves passage from each phase to the next.

The canonical lifecycle runs initiation (the idea becomes a proposal — everything this course has taught so far is initiation work), planning (the workplan package of Section 6.8), execution (the science happens), monitoring and controlling (running continuously alongside execution — Section 6.6), and closure (terminal reports, liquidation, asset disposition, and the too-often-forgotten harvest: data archived, papers submitted, IP disclosed per Chapter 5). Funded research inserts phase gates at the joints: proposal approval, budget release, midterm review, extension decisions, terminal acceptance — each a point where the funder can redirect or stop the work, and each mapped to a document your team must produce.

Two vocabulary distinctions do real work in workplans. A deliverable is a thing handed over — the calibrated dataset, the trained-teachers cohort, the commissioning report; a milestone is a point in time marking verifiable progress — "all five stations installed," "midterm review passed." Deliverables consume budget; milestones consume nothing but prove everything, which is why funders schedule payments against them. And a work package — the term your budget will lean on — is the smallest chunk of scope assigned to one owner with its own schedule and cost: the atom of accountability.

Common Pitfall
Treating closure as an afterthought. Liquidation delays and unarchived data are the classic endings of Philippine grants — and an unliquidated project can bar its leader and institution from the next grant. Schedule closure as a work package with an owner, not as whatever remains in the final month.

6.3From Objectives to Schedule: WBS, Gantt, Critical Path

Chapter 2 built the objectives tree downward from agendas; planning now decomposes it further — into work, then into time.

The work breakdown structure (WBS) answers one question exhaustively: what work, in pieces small enough to own? Each specific objective decomposes into work packages, each work package into activities; the discipline is the 100% rule — the children must sum to the whole of the parent, no orphan work and no phantom work. The WBS is deliberately timeless; only when it is complete do you sequence it. Then the Gantt chart lays activities on the calendar with their dependencies (activity B cannot start until A finishes — site MOU before installation; ethics clearance before interviews, as Chapters 1 and 4 insisted). The longest dependent chain through the network is the critical path: the sequence with zero slack, whose delay delays the project one-for-one. Everything not on the critical path has float — schedule flexibility you spend deliberately, not accidentally.

Key Concept
The traceability chain of Chapters 1–2 now has its middle links forged: objective → work package → activity → time (Gantt) and cost (budget). The WBS is the junction where science becomes management — every budget line and every schedule bar must name its work package.

6.4The Line-Item Budget: PS, MOOE, CO — and the Proportions That Betray You

Philippine public money is planned and audited in a fixed vocabulary. Learn it once and every government budget document — including the one in your Case Analysis — becomes legible.

Every peso in a government-funded project belongs to an expense class: Personal Services (PS) — salaries, wages, and honoraria of project personnel; Maintenance and Other Operating Expenses (MOOE) — the consumable life of the project: supplies and materials, travel, communications, utilities, repairs, training costs, publication charges; and Capital Outlay (CO, in research grants usually Equipment Outlay) — durable assets above the capitalization threshold: the telescope, the server, the vehicle. The classification is not pedantry: each class carries different rules for procurement, accounting, and — as Chapter 5's Prof. Son learned — ownership and disposition: equipment bought as CO is government property whose fate at project's end follows the grant's rules, not the project leader's wishes.

Above the classes sits a second cut: direct costs — everything attributable to the research itself (the PS, MOOE, and CO of the work packages) — versus indirect costs — the institution's overhead for hosting the project: administration, utilities, facilities. Funding rules permit indirect costs only as a modest, capped percentage of direct costs, because public research money is appropriated to buy research, not to underwrite institutional overhead at scale. This single principle decides your Case Analysis budget item: a proposal whose "indirect cost" dwarfs its direct cost has inverted the architecture — it asks the funder to buy mostly overhead, garnished with a little science.

Costing Honestly: How the Numbers Are Born

A defensible budget is derived, not declared. The procedure: take each work package from the WBS; list the people it needs (→ PS: rates × person-months), the consumables and services it burns (→ MOOE: unit costs × quantities, with canvassed prices, not guesses), and the durable assets it requires (→ CO: specified models with quotations); sum upward. The result inherits the traceability that reviewers check — every line answers "for which activity, toward which objective?" — and produces the two artifacts that accompany serious budgets: unit costing (the arithmetic shown, not just totals) and the budget-by-work-package cross-table that proves the triangle coheres. Two more lines complete Philippine practice: counterpart funding — the proponent institution's visible contribution (staff time, facilities, sometimes cash), evidence of institutional commitment (Chapter 2's HNRDA discussion promised this would return); and the protection and compliance lines Chapters 4–5 taught you to include: permit fees, monitoring costs, IPOPHL fees.

Common Pitfall
Padding — inflating lines "for safety" — and its mirror, heroic underbudgeting to look cheap. Both are detected the same way: the reviewer prices a few lines independently. The honest instrument for uncertainty is a visible contingency where rules allow it, and a risk register (Sec. 6.7) explaining what it covers — not fog distributed across every line.
Astronomy in Focus
Instrument budgets have famous hidden lines: shipping and customs for imported optics; spares (a CCD that fails in Month 18 with no replacement kills a time-series); calibration references; site electrification; insurance for deployed equipment; and the GPS-disciplined clocks RA 10535 taught you to trace. Walk your WBS asking "what does this physically require?" — the sky-facing budget failures are almost always MOOE lines nobody imagined.

6.5Spending Lawfully: Procurement, Audit, Liquidation

A funded budget is not money you have; it is public money you are accountable for. Three institutions define that accountability.

Procurement. Government funds buy through the public procurement regime — long governed by RA 9184 (the Government Procurement Reform Act of 2003) and now carried by the New Government Procurement Act (RA 12009, 2024) — whose default is competitive public bidding, with alternative modalities (small-value procurement, shopping, direct contracting for proprietary items, negotiated procurement in defined cases) available only on stated grounds. For a project manager the operational meaning is lead time: a bid-procured spectrograph can take many months from purchase request to delivery, which is why procurement sat on the critical path of Figure 6.3 — and why Prof. Son's Project UWU, delayed by "the very slow procurement process," is a scheduling case as much as a legal one. Managers who survive procurement do three things: specify early (the technical specifications, not the brand, define what bidding buys), bundle sensibly, and start the paperwork the week the grant is signed.

Audit. The Commission on Audit (COA) — a constitutional body — examines every peso of public spending after the fact: was the expense lawful, budgeted, documented, and reasonable? Its instruments are the audit observation memorandum and, at worst, the notice of disallowance — a finding that money must be returned, personally, by the officials who approved it. The manager's defense is boring and absolute: documentation — canvasses, purchase orders, inspection and acceptance reports, attendance sheets for that training MOOE line, and a paper trail matching each expense to its budget line and work package.

Liquidation. Funds released to a project are advances to be liquidated: reported, receipted, and reconciled within the grant's deadlines. Unliquidated balances block subsequent releases, poison institutional standing with the funder, and outlive projects as personal liabilities. The closure work package of Figure 6.2 is, concretely, this: liquidate, transfer or dispose equipment per the grant's property rules, and file the terminal report that gates the funder's acceptance.

Key Concept
Chapter 3's mandates/money/standards/constraints framework, applied inward: your own project's money arrives wrapped in constraints (procurement law), standards (accounting rules), and a mandate (deliver the science). The statute-reading skill now audits you.

6.6Monitoring and Evaluation: The Logframe at Work

Chapter 2 introduced the results chain as policy architecture; here it becomes your project's nervous system.

Keep the two words distinct. Monitoring is continuous and internal: tracking implementation against plan — are activities on schedule, is the budget burning at the planned rate, are outputs emerging? Its instruments are the Gantt-versus-actual comparison, the financial utilization report, and the indicator dashboard. Evaluation is periodic and judgmental: at gates and at the end, asking whether the project worked — did outputs appear (terminal evaluation), are outcomes emerging (post-project), was the design itself right (the hardest question)? Monitoring corrects the steering; evaluation grades the voyage and teaches the next one.

The instrument that disciplines both is the logical framework (logframe) — the results chain (inputs → activities → outputs → outcomes → impact) written as a matrix. Each level states its objectively verifiable indicators (how much, by when — the SMART discipline of Chapter 1 applied to progress), its means of verification (the document or dataset where a third party checks the indicator — the same verifiable-objects standard that has run through every chapter), and its assumptions — the conditions outside your control that must hold for this level to produce the next. The assumptions column is the logframe's philosophical heart and the most-skipped cell in student workplans: it is where honest projects admit what they are betting on (clear skies, LGU cooperation, timely procurement), and it feeds the risk register directly.

Key Concept
An indicator without a means of verification is an opinion with a number attached. The MOV column is this course's specificity-causality-honesty test built into a table — if no document could prove the indicator, the indicator is decoration.
For the Proposal
Funders increasingly ask for outputs in 6Ps language (Ch. 1): make each P a logframe output row with its own indicator and MOV — publications (DOI count), people services (certified trainees), places & partnerships (signed MOUs). The vocabulary crosswalk costs one afternoon and reads as fluency.

6.7Risk Management: The Register, the Matrix, the Four Responses

Every assumption in the logframe is a bet. Risk management is the discipline of naming the bets, sizing them, and deciding in advance what you will do when the sky — literal or bureaucratic — refuses to cooperate.

The working instrument is the risk register: a living table where each risk gets an owner, a probability rating, an impact rating, and a planned response. Probability × impact locates each risk on the prioritization matrix — attention flows to the red corner, not to whichever risk was discussed most recently. The four canonical responses: avoid (redesign so the risk cannot occur — move the site out of the flood zone), reduce (lower probability or impact — spares on the shelf, two suppliers qualified, early procurement), transfer (make it someone else's risk — insurance on deployed instruments, fixed-price contracts), and accept (for low-priority risks: document, monitor, and budget the contingency of Section 6.4). R&D's characteristic risks cluster predictably: procurement delay (the Philippine classic — Project UWU's downfall and the critical path's usual occupant), technical uncertainty (the detector underperforms — contained by pilot phases and go/no-go gates), people (the trained RA graduates and leaves — contained by documentation and cross-training, and by the retention economics Chapter 7's Magna Carta addresses), site and weather (habagat, typhoons, land access), and partner drift (the LGU's priorities change with its administration — the assumptions column made flesh).

Key Concept
The register's power is pre-commitment: responses decided calmly in planning execute quickly in crisis. A project that discovers its response during the crisis pays for the same decision twice — once in delay, once in panic.
Common Pitfall
The write-once register. A risk register dated the same day as the proposal and never touched again is a compliance ornament. Reviews at each gate (Fig. 6.2) re-score probabilities, retire dead risks, and add the ones execution taught you — the register is a diary, not a foreword.

6.8Synthesis: Assembling the Workplan Package

Week 12 will ask you for a workplan. This section is its packing list — and the map of how every chapter so far becomes a section of one coherent document.

Two management rhythms animate the package after approval. The reporting calendar: progress reports at the funder's intervals, financial reports aligned to liquidation deadlines, and the terminal report as the closure gate — each drawing its content directly from the logframe's indicators and the Gantt-versus-actual record, which is why teams that monitor well write reports quickly and teams that don't relive the whole project each reporting season. And the change discipline: when reality diverges from plan — it will — changes to scope, schedule, or budget above the funder's thresholds require prior approval, realignments between budget lines follow the grant's rules, and extensions must be requested before expiry with cause shown (a no-cost extension stretches time without new money — the standard remedy for a procurement-delayed but scientifically sound project, and the correct reading of Project UWU's situation). The worst change management is silence: funders forgive documented adversity far more readily than discovered surprises.

Close with the standing move — the compressed-IRAC paragraph for your proposal's management section: "(R) The grant's terms require milestone-based reporting, procurement under RA 12009, liquidation per deadline, and prior approval for major realignments; (A) the project operates the package above — logframe-driven quarterly reports, procurement initiated at Day 1 per the critical path, a gate-reviewed risk register, and a dated compliance calendar with named owners; (C) the project's management system therefore meets the grant's terms and makes its own performance auditable." The panel reads that paragraph and sees the thing it funds most confidently: a team that has already imagined Month 14.

Astronomy in Focus
The complete TE01 arc is now visible: a research question (Ch. 1) aligned to agendas (Ch. 2), sited within the law (Chs. 3–4), owning its outputs (Ch. 5), and managed through this chapter's package — which is precisely the R&D proposal your final requirement demands and your Innovative Pitching must sell in Week 18.
Key Concept
The chapter in one sentence: decompose (WBS), schedule (Gantt), cost (PS/MOOE/CO with capped indirect), spend lawfully (procure–document–liquidate), measure (logframe), pre-decide (risk register), and report — with every artifact tracing to the same objectives.

Question 1 · Applying

Midway through your project, the funder asks you to add two more monitoring sites "within the same grant." Under the triple constraint, the correct managerial response is:

  1. Agree silently; funders are always right
  2. Show the triangle: added scope must move time and/or cost — so either the schedule extends, the budget grows, or existing scope is traded out; agreeing without adjusting any of the three quietly promises to break quality
  3. Refuse all changes on principle
  4. Add the sites but stop calibrating to save time
Show correct answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. The constraint is arithmetic, not attitude: scope, time, and cost are coupled, and the professional answer to a scope request is a costed change proposal, not heroic silence. D names the hidden victim — quality — explicitly; it is what absorbs incoherent promises, in this case the calibration that made the data trustworthy at all.

Question 2 · Analyzing

What distinguishes well-managed R&D from a construction project, per Section 6.1?

  1. R&D needs no schedule because science is unpredictable
  2. The scientific outcome is irreducibly uncertain — but good management confines uncertainty to that outcome, running schedule, spending, data, and compliance as tightly as any bridge; the panel's feasibility question is whether the team has achieved that confinement
  3. Construction has budgets; research does not
  4. Nothing; they are identical
Show correct answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. The Frascati "uncertain" criterion is a fact about the science, not a license for the management. A is the romantic fallacy that kills grants: unpredictable results still emerge from predictable campaigns, procured equipment, and monitored budgets. The confinement idea is the chapter's master metaphor — watch it reappear in the risk register.

Question 3 · Applying

Classify: (1) "calibrated first-light images delivered to the repository"; (2) "all five stations installed"; (3) "conduct monthly sky-brightness campaigns." In lifecycle vocabulary these are:

  1. 1 deliverable · 2 milestone · 3 activity
  2. 1 milestone · 2 activity · 3 deliverable
  3. All three are milestones
  4. 1 activity · 2 deliverable · 3 milestone
Show correct answer & explanation

Correct answer: A. A deliverable is a thing handed over (images in the repository); a milestone is a verifiable point in time (installation complete — nothing handed over, everything proven); an activity is work that consumes time and budget (the campaigns). Funders schedule payments against milestones precisely because they are checkable instants — mislabeling them muddles your payment schedule.

Question 4 · Analyzing

A WBS for a five-station network omits "site host agreements" but includes "celebrate project anniversary." The two defects, named precisely, are:

  1. Both are style choices
  2. A 100%-rule violation in each direction: orphan work (site agreements are real, necessary work missing from the decomposition — it will be done unbudgeted and unscheduled) and phantom work (the celebration serves no objective and will consume budget that traces to nothing)
  3. The WBS is too detailed
  4. Site agreements belong in the celebration package
Show correct answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. The 100% rule cuts both ways: children must sum to the parent — no missing work (orphans surface later as schedule shocks and unfunded effort) and no extra work (phantoms are the padding audits find). The WBS review question is always the pair: what real work is absent, and what present work serves no objective?

Question 5 · Applying

In Figure 6.3, training floats while procurement→install→commission is critical. A team member proposes borrowing the training slot's staff to "help chase procurement." The schedule-literate evaluation is:

  1. Never move anyone; plans are sacred
  2. Sensible use of float — training can slip within its slack without moving the milestone, while any acceleration of the critical path shortens the whole project; spending float deliberately on the zero-slack chain is exactly what float is for
  3. Pointless — all activities are equally urgent
  4. Wrong — training is always the critical path
Show correct answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. Critical-path literacy is resource literacy: a day saved off-path saves nothing, a day saved on-path saves a project-day. The float is a budget of flexibility — spent deliberately (and visibly, so the training milestone is re-dated), not leaked accidentally. C is the flat-schedule fallacy the method exists to dissolve.

Question 6 · Applying

Classify these budget lines: (1) honoraria of the project's field observers; (2) a ₱1.8M spectrograph; (3) fuel and ferry fares for site visits; (4) publication charges for the open-access paper.

  1. 1 PS · 2 CO/EO · 3 MOOE · 4 MOOE
  2. 1 MOOE · 2 MOOE · 3 CO · 4 PS
  3. All four are Capital Outlay
  4. 1 CO · 2 PS · 3 PS · 4 CO
Show correct answer & explanation

Correct answer: A. People are PS (salaries, wages, honoraria); durable assets above the threshold are CO/EO (the spectrograph — which becomes government property with disposition rules); the consumable life of the project — travel, fuel, fees, page charges — is MOOE. The classification decides procurement route, audit treatment, and end-of-project ownership, which is why examiners test it with lists exactly like this.

Question 7 · Evaluating

Your Case Analysis: STARI requests ₱2.5B — direct costs ₱500M (PS 200, MOOE 200, CO 100) and "indirect cost" ₱2.0B. The correct diagnosis and repair is:

  1. Approve; the arithmetic sums correctly
  2. The proportion is inverted: indirect costs are permitted only as a modest, capped percentage OF direct costs, not four times them — the request buys mostly overhead; repair by re-deriving the budget from the WBS (cost each work package into PS/MOOE/CO), summing the true direct cost, and adding indirect at the funder's capped rate on top
  3. Reject because the PS share is too small
  4. Move the ₱2B to CO and approve
Show correct answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. Summing correctly is not budgeting correctly: the architecture requires direct costs to dominate, with overhead riding capped on top, because public research money is appropriated to buy research. D relabels the pathology without curing it — ₱2B of unspecified "equipment" fails the same traceability test. This is the mastery version of the 100-point case item; the repair procedure is as gradeable as the diagnosis.

Question 8 · Analyzing

Why do funders require counterpart funding from the proponent institution?

  1. To make small institutions ineligible
  2. As revealed commitment: an institution that stakes its own staff time, facilities, or cash has skin in the project's success — and the counterpart also evidences the institutional capacity (labs, admin support) the project silently relies on
  3. Because grants are loans
  4. To complicate the spreadsheet
Show correct answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. Counterpart is an incentive-alignment instrument (the same design logic as RA 10055's revenue sharing): commitments are more credible when costly. It also surfaces the true cost of research — the institutional infrastructure that "free" hosting actually provides — which is the honest cousin of the indirect-cost line.

Question 9 · Applying

Your project needs a ₱1.6M custom telescope mount. The procurement-literate plan is:

  1. Buy it from the project leader's cousin for speed
  2. Route it through the public procurement regime (RA 12009, ex-RA 9184): write performance-based technical specifications early, anticipate competitive bidding's full lead time on the critical path, and invoke an alternative modality only on grounds the law actually provides (e.g., direct contracting for a genuinely proprietary item)
  3. Split it into ₱49,999 pieces to dodge thresholds
  4. Pay cash and keep the receipts in a shoebox
Show correct answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. Public money buys through public procedure: bidding is the default, alternatives are exceptions with stated grounds, and the manager's real lever is time — early, brand-neutral specs and Day-1 paperwork. Question 10 · Analyzing

A COA notice of disallowance differs from an ordinary budget disagreement because it:

  1. Is merely advisory
  2. Finds public money improperly spent and requires its return — with personal liability attaching to the approving and certifying officials; the shield against it is not eloquence at audit time but documentation built at expense time
  3. Applies only to private companies
  4. Can be settled by revising next year's proposal
Show correct answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. Disallowance is restitution, not critique — the constitutional audit power reaching into personal bank accounts of signatories. The managerial consequence is the boring habit the chapter preaches: every expense matched at birth to its budget line, work package, and supporting papers. Auditors reconstruct trails; good managers never let the trail need reconstruction.

Question 11 · Analyzing

Distinguish monitoring from evaluation with the correct instrument pairing:

  1. Monitoring: continuous tracking of implementation against plan (Gantt-vs-actual, burn rate, indicator dashboard); evaluation: periodic judgment at gates and end — did outputs appear, are outcomes emerging, was the design right
  2. Monitoring is what funders do; evaluation is what teams do
  3. They are synonyms used to lengthen reports
  4. Evaluation is continuous; monitoring happens once
Show correct answer & explanation

Correct answer: A. Monitoring corrects the steering; evaluation grades the voyage. The tense differs (present-continuous vs. retrospective), the questions differ (on plan? vs. worked?), and the consumers differ (the team daily; the funder and the next project's designers at gates). Question 12 · Evaluating

Two logframe indicator rows for the same output: Row X — "Community engagement improved." Row Y — "20 barangay observers certified by Month 12; MOV: certification records and practical-exam sheets." The decisive difference is:

  1. X is shorter, hence better
  2. Y is verifiable: quantity, deadline, and a named document a third party can check — while X has no number, no date, and no MOV, making it an opinion wearing an indicator's clothes; the SMART and specificity-causality-honesty tests, now enforced by a table
  3. Both are equally valid styles
  4. Y is worse because certification is bureaucratic

Correct answer: B. The MOV column is the logframe's honesty mechanism: an indicator no document could prove is decoration. This is the fourth domain where the course's verifiability standard has appeared (agendas, statutes, compliance, now M&E) — by design, because panels apply it everywhere too.

Question 13 · Analyzing

The logframe's assumptions column exists because:

  1. Templates abhor empty space
  2. Each level produces the next only IF conditions outside the project's control hold — naming them makes the causal logic honest, tells monitoring what to watch, and feeds the risk register the bets it must manage; a logframe with an empty assumptions column claims omnipotence
  3. Assumptions are excuses prepared in advance
  4. Funders never read that column

Correct answer: B. The if-then-given logic (if activities AND assumptions → outputs) is what separates a logframe from a wish list. Question 14 · Applying

Match the risk response: (1) insure the deployed stations; (2) reschedule campaigns away from habagat months; (3) qualify two suppliers and start procurement at Day 1; (4) note minor price drift and cover it from contingency.

  1. 1 transfer · 2 avoid · 3 reduce · 4 accept
  2. 1 avoid · 2 transfer · 3 accept · 4 reduce
  3. All four are "accept"
  4. 1 reduce · 2 accept · 3 avoid · 4 transfer
Show correct answer & explanation

Correct answer: A. Insurance moves the loss to another balance sheet (transfer); scheduling around the cloud season redesigns the exposure out of existence (avoid); dual suppliers and early paperwork lower the probability and impact of the classic delay (reduce); documented low-priority risks with a funded contingency are accepted, not ignored. Four verbs, four different managerial acts — the register should name which one each row performs.

Question 15 · Evaluating

Project UWU is two years in, scientifically sound, but behind schedule because of the university's slow procurement. As its manager approaching expiry, your correct instrument and argument is:

  1. Say nothing and hope the funder forgets the end date
  2. Request a no-cost extension before expiry: cause shown (documented procurement delays on the critical path), remaining work and revised Gantt attached, no new funds sought — the standard, legitimate remedy for exactly this situation, and far cheaper in credibility than discovered silence
  3. Declare the project finished and skip the undone work
  4. Move the project to a faster university mid-grant on your own authority
Show correct answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. The no-cost extension is change discipline working as designed: time moves, money doesn't, and the request precedes expiry with cause and a plan. A converts adversity into concealment (the unforgivable version); C falsifies completion; D is the Prof. Son fallacy from Chapter 5 — the grant is a GFA–RDI agreement no individual can relocate. Note how the risk register would have made this request's evidence file automatic.

Question 16 · Applying

Your quarterly report is due. The fastest honest source of its content is:

  1. Memory and adjectives
  2. The monitoring system you already run: Gantt-versus-actual for schedule, utilization-versus-plan for budget, the logframe's indicators with their MOVs for results, and the risk register's updates for issues — reporting as extraction, not creation
  3. Last quarter's report with the dates changed
  4. A request for deadline extension every quarter
Show correct answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. Teams that monitor well write reports quickly because the report is a view of a running system, not a quarterly archaeology expedition. Question 17 · Analyzing

Why does the chapter insist the budget be derived from the WBS rather than written directly?

  1. Spreadsheets require it technically
  2. Because derivation creates traceability in both directions: every line answers "for which activity, toward which objective," and every work package shows its true cost — which is what lets reviewers walk the chain, auditors match expenses, and managers see instantly what any scope change costs
  3. To make budgets longer
  4. Tradition alone
Show correct answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. A declared budget is a set of hopeful numbers; a derived budget is the WBS priced. The derivation is also your change-management calculator (Question 1's costed response comes from it) and your padding detector — lines that trace to no work package are phantoms by construction. The chain from Chapter 1's "reviewers read budgets backwards" now has its full machinery.

Question 18 · Applying

Your CO-funded 60-cm telescope reaches project end. Its correct disposition is decided by:

  1. The project leader's sentimental attachment
  2. The grant's property and disposition rules — CO equipment is government property, typically transferred, retained, or disposed per the funder's guidelines and accounting procedure, documented in the closure package; not by possession, affection, or a new employer's welcome letter
  3. Whoever physically holds the keys at midnight on the end date
  4. A raffle among project staff
Show correct answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. The Chapter 5 separation (IP vs. chattel vs. grant relationship) closes here on the chattel side: equipment follows property rules through the closure gate, with inspection, inventory, and transfer documents COA can audit. This is also the final answer to the Prof. Son equipment question — and why closure is a work package, not an afterthought.

Question 19 · Evaluating

Two management sections reach a panel. Section X: "The team will manage the project diligently and report regularly." Section Y: "Quarterly logframe-based reports; procurement initiated Day 1 (critical path, Fig. 2); risk register reviewed at each gate (owners named); liquidation 30 days before deadline; compliance calendar attached." The panel's reading:

  1. X wins on brevity
  2. Y demonstrates a management system — named instruments, dates, owners, and artifacts a monitor can check — while X restates the word "manage" in adjectives; the unfalsifiable-sentence test, applied to its fifth domain
  3. Both are equivalent
  4. Y is worse because it admits risks exist
Show correct answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. By now the pattern is reflex: verifiable objects beat sentiments in agendas, statutes, compliance, IP, and management alike. D deserves its own burial — admitting risks with responses attached is precisely what distinguishes a team that has imagined Month 14 from a team that will meet it unarmed.

Question 20 · Creating / Evaluating

Synthesis: a reviewer picks one peso from your budget — a ₱12,000 line for GPS-disciplined clock modules — and asks you to justify it completely. The full-course answer traces:

  1. "Electronics are always useful."
  2. Line → work package 1.4 (commissioning) → objective 1 (network operational) → logframe output indicator (five stations, PhST-stamped uptime logs as MOV) → methodological requirement (multi-station triangulation needs traceable time, RA 10535) → agenda anchor (DRR-relevant space science) — one peso, walked backward to the mission and forward to a verifiable result
  3. "The supplier had a promotion."
  4. "It was left over from another line."
Show correct answer & explanation

Correct answer: B. The single-peso walk is the course's entire argument in miniature: Chapter 1's design chain, Chapter 2's alignment, Chapter 3's standards statute, Chapter 6's WBS-derived budget and logframe — all meeting in one defensible line item.
