5.1Knowledge as an Asset: Why R&D Managers Study IP

Chapter 1 ended with a warning that R&D is not yet innovation; something must carry a result out of the laboratory and into use. Intellectual property is the legal form that carrying takes: it converts knowledge — non-rivalrous, copyable, leaky — into an asset that can be owned, licensed, invested in, and shared on terms.

The economic logic is a deliberate bargain. Knowledge is expensive to produce and nearly free to copy; without some protection, private investment in invention under-supplies. The State therefore grants inventors and creators limited, temporary exclusivity — and takes, in exchange, full public disclosure (patents are published precisely so others can learn from them) and eventual expiry into the public domain. The system is thus not the opposite of open science; it is a differently-timed openness, and managing the timing is most of what an R&D manager's "IP strategy" means. The bargain has honest critics — exclusivity can price out the poor, patent thickets can slow research, and most university patents never earn back their fees — and a competent manager knows both the tool and its limits.

Key Concept
IP is timed openness: disclosure now, exclusivity for a term, public domain forever after. Your strategy decisions are mostly sequencing decisions — what to disclose, when, in which order relative to filing and publishing.
Astronomy in Focus
Astronomy looks IP-poor (you cannot own a galaxy) and is actually IP-rich in the engineering layer: detector designs, mount mechanisms, adaptive-optics methods, control software, calibration pipelines. The science stays open; the instruments that produce it are where the field's patents live — a split your own proposal will reproduce in miniature.

5.2The IP Code (RA 8293) and the Family of Rights

The Intellectual Property Code of the Philippines — RA 8293 (1997), since amended — consolidated the country's IP law into one statute and created the Intellectual Property Office of the Philippines (IPOPHL) to administer it. Its structure is a family of distinct rights, each matched to a kind of creation, each with its own requirements and clock.

Reading the Family Strategically

The rights differ on three axes worth memorizing. How protection arises: copyright is automatic on creation and trade secrecy arises from conduct, while patents, utility models, designs, and trademarks exist only through registration with IPOPHL. What is protected: patents protect function (the technical solution itself, however expressed); copyright protects expression (this code, this text — not the idea behind it); designs protect appearance; trademarks protect commercial identity; secrets protect whatever secrecy can actually keep. The clock: from the utility model's short, unrenewable seven years to the trademark's potentially eternal life (renewed decade by decade, kept alive by actual use), each term expresses a policy judgment about how long exclusivity should pay for disclosure.

Employee and commissioned work deserves early attention, because a university team is full of both. As a default under the Code, an invention made by an employee as part of regularly assigned duties belongs to the employer (absent contrary agreement), while an invention outside those duties stays with the employee even if company time or materials were used; copyright in works made in the course of employment follows related default rules. RA 10055 (Section 5.5) then overlays the government-funding dimension. The lesson is not the defaults themselves but their fragility: every research engagement should say, in writing, who will own what — the sentence that prevents most academic IP disputes ever occurring.

PH Context
The Philippines is plugged into the international IP system: the Paris Convention (priority rights), the Patent Cooperation Treaty (one international patent application preserving rights in most of the world), the Madrid Protocol (international trademark registration), and the Berne Convention (automatic copyright abroad). A Filipino invention can go global through Philippine filings — the on-ramp is IPOPHL.
Common Pitfall
Confusing plagiarism with infringement. Plagiarism is an ethical wrong (claiming credit) policed by institutions; infringement is a legal wrong (violating an exclusive right) policed by courts. Quoting with attribution can still infringe; copying public-domain text without attribution is plagiarism but not infringement. Your Case Analysis answers must keep the two vocabularies apart.

5.3Patents: The Three Tests, the Exclusions, and the Journey

A patent is the strongest right in the family — a twenty-year exclusivity over a technical solution to a problem — and correspondingly the hardest to earn. Everything turns on three statutory tests and one list of exclusions.

The Three Tests

An invention is patentable if it is new (not part of the prior art — everything made available to the public anywhere in the world before the filing date, including your own conference talk); involves an inventive step (not obvious to a person skilled in the art, given the prior art — the test that separates real invention from routine engineering); and is industrially applicable (can be made or used in some industry — almost never the obstacle). Novelty is brutally objective: one enabling disclosure anywhere, by anyone, including the inventor, destroys it. The Code softens this with a twelve-month grace period — a disclosure derived from the inventor within twelve months before filing does not count against novelty — but the grace protects you only from your own disclosures, not from a rival who published independently in the meantime, and many foreign jurisdictions are less forgiving. Professional practice therefore treats the grace period as a seatbelt, not a driving style: file before you speak.

The Journey, and the First-to-File Rule

The Philippines is a first-to-file jurisdiction: between two independent inventors, the patent belongs to the earlier application, not the earlier invention — which is why laboratory notebooks matter less for priority here than filing discipline does. The application itself must enable: it discloses the invention completely enough for a skilled person to carry it out, and its claims define the legal boundary of the exclusivity (the claims, not the prose, are what competitors will read). The office journey then runs: filing (a date that fixes everything) → formality examination → publication at eighteen months (your secret becomes public whether or not a patent ever issues — a strategic fact) → substantive examination upon request → grant or refusal, with post-grant maintenance fees keeping the right alive. A pending application already carries provisional protection against copyists once published.

The utility model deserves its own strategic paragraph. It protects the same kind of subject matter as a patent but is registered without inventive-step examination — faster, far cheaper, and correspondingly weaker and shorter (seven years, no renewal). For the incremental engineering that dominates university instrument work — a better mount clamp, a cleverer baffle, a low-cost drive — the utility model is often the honest right: the improvement is new and useful but might not survive an obviousness challenge, and seven years exceeds the commercial life of most laboratory hardware anyway. Filipino university ITSOs (Section 5.6) push utility models hard for exactly these reasons.

Astronomy in Focus
Run the exclusion clause against your own work: the exoplanet you detect, the period-luminosity relation you fit, the dark-matter profile you infer — discoveries and theories, unpatentable by design, and rightly so; they are the commons your science feeds. The spectrograph configuration, the detector cooling method, the observing-scheduler embodied in a running system — those are candidate inventions. The line between finding and making is the line between Sections 22 and 21.
Common Pitfall
The thesis-defense novelty suicide: a public defense, an uploaded abstract, or a conference poster is prior art against your own later filing. The grace period may save a Philippine application; it will not always save a foreign one. If an invention matters, the invention disclosure goes in before the abstract goes out.

5.4Copyright, Software, and Data: The Rights You Already Own

While patents must be won, copyright simply happens: the moment your paper, code, figure, or lecture pack is fixed in some medium, the right exists — no registration, no fee, no notice required (registration and deposit exist and help as evidence, but they do not create the right).

Copyright protects original expression, and the Code lists computer programs among protected literary works — which means every reduction pipeline, scheduler, and control script your project writes is born copyrighted, with economic rights (reproduction, adaptation, distribution) that last in general for the author's life plus fifty years, and moral rights (attribution, integrity) that Philippine law takes seriously. What copyright does not protect matters just as much to a scientist: ideas, procedures, methods, discoveries, and data as such. The measured magnitudes in your catalog are facts — free for all — even though your database's original selection and arrangement, your prose describing it, and your plots are protected expression. There is no separate database right in Philippine law; a well-chosen license does the work instead.

This is where Chapter 2's open science returns wearing legal clothes. Open licensing is copyright, exercised — a Creative Commons license on your data paper, an OSI-approved license on your pipeline, are grants of permission that only an owner can give. The practical sequence for a project: know what you own (code, text, figures — automatically), decide what the funder and the UNESCO-era data-management plan require to be open, and attach the license that says so explicitly. Unlicensed public code is not open science; it is legal ambiguity with a repository URL. Note also works of the government: Philippine law places official government works outside copyright's usual grip (with conditions), one more reason publicly funded outputs default toward openness.

Key Concept
The scientist's copyright triangle: facts are free; expression is owned; licenses are how owners share. Every dataset release decision is a walk around this triangle — and your data-management plan is its documentation.
For the Proposal
Write the licensing table now, not at publication: for each expected output (pipeline, catalog, paper, teaching modules), one row naming the owner (per RA 10055 — next section), the license, and the release timing. Five rows, one page, and both the open-science reviewer and the IP reviewer are satisfied at once.

5.5RA 10055: The Philippine Technology Transfer Act

Now the statute your Case Analysis has been circling. Before 2010, IP from government-funded research sat in an ownership fog — formally with funding agencies that had no capacity to commercialize, practically with no one — and inventions died in reports. RA 10055 (the Philippine Technology Transfer Act of 2009, signed 2010), modeled on the American Bayh-Dole Act, dispersed the fog with one central rule.

The rule: ownership vests in the research development institution. IP generated from research funded by government funding agencies (GFAs) — DOST and its councils, other agencies — belongs as a default to the research development institution (RDI) that actually performed the work: the university, the government research institute, the lab. The theory is managerial, not sentimental: the performing institution is closest to the invention, best placed to protect and commercialize it, and most motivated when it owns the result. In exchange, the Act loads the RDI with duties and reserves rights to the public:

  • Disclosure and diligence — the RDI must have researchers disclose inventions, must decide whether to protect them, and must actually pursue commercialization rather than warehouse rights;
  • Government reserved rights — the GFA keeps a royalty-free license to use the IP for government purposes, and march-in style powers let government act (require licensing) in extreme cases: national emergency, public interest, or the RDI's failure to commercialize within a reasonable time;
  • Revenue sharing — commercialization income is shared between the RDI and the researchers who created the IP, on terms fixed by institutional policy under the Act and its IRR — the researcher's legal right to a fair share of what their invention earns;
  • Commercialization freedom — RDIs may license, assign, or form spin-off companies, and researchers may be allowed equity or roles in them, within conflict-of-interest safeguards.

Reading the Act Into Your Case Analysis

Your Case Analysis scenarios about Prof. Lahk Son's Project UWU are RA 10055 questions wearing narrative clothes, and the Act's machinery answers them in IRAC form. Who owns the instrument produced by a DOST-funded university project? Distinguish the layers first: the IP (the invention embodied in the instrument) vests by default in the RDI — the university that performed the research — not in the funding agency and not in the project leader personally; the physical equipment bought with project funds follows the funding agency's property and accounting rules (the DOST-GIA guidelines you will read in Week 10), which is a different question with a different answer. Can a departing project leader take the project, team, and equipment to another university? The grant is an agreement between the GFA and the RDI — not the leader's personal property — so moving it requires the parties' consent, not a resignation letter. Can protection be applied for during an extension period? The duty to disclose and protect attaches to IP generated from the funded research whenever it arises within the project's legal life — which is exactly why you must read the grant agreement and the IRR rather than assume. Notice the recurring analytic move: separate the IP from the chattel from the grant relationship, then apply each body of rules to its own object.

Key Concept
RA 10055's genius is the alignment of incentives: the institution that can act owns; the inventors who created share; the public that paid keeps a safety valve. When a case scenario feels confusing, ask which of the three interests is being violated — the answer usually names the section you need.
PH Context
The Act works in tandem with the Magna Carta you will study next chapter: RA 10055 lets researchers earn from inventions; RA 8439/11312 ensures they are paid fairly as scientists. Together they answer the brain-drain problem with money where the talent is — remember this pairing when the Week 10 cases ask about researcher compensation.

5.6Technology Transfer in Practice: Instruments, Agreements, Offices

Ownership is potential; transfer is motion. The working instruments of technology transfer are a small set of agreements every project manager should recognize on sight — plus the institutional offices built to wield them.

Choosing the Instrument

The choice tracks two questions: who should own, and how wide should use be. Keep ownership and license non-exclusively when the technology is an enabling method many users should adopt (a calibration technique, an open dataset's commercial mirror); license exclusively, bounded by field and territory, when a single licensee must invest heavily to bring the product to market and needs protection to justify it (an instrument manufacturer tooling up a production line); assign almost never with publicly funded IP — an assignment is irreversible, and RA 10055's diligence-and-reserved-rights architecture presumes the RDI stays owner. MTAs and NDAs are the humble instruments that prevent disasters: the NDA lets you talk to a manufacturer before filing without destroying novelty; the MTA moves a coated sample or detector chip with its ownership, use limits, and publication rights spelled out. Every one of these is a place where the IP Code's TTA rules apply — Philippine law polices the fairness of transfer terms, prohibiting clauses that would shackle the licensee's research freedom or coerce back improvements, a consumer-protection instinct applied to knowledge markets.

For the Proposal
Name your ITSO (or the office performing that function) in the proposal's IP section, with the disclosure-to-decision workflow of Section 5.8. Reviewers read an unnamed "we will protect our IP" the way they read "we will obey all laws" (Ch. 4): as a sentence that cannot be false and therefore says nothing.
Common Pitfall
Signing the collaborator's "standard" agreement unread. Foreign partners' templates routinely assign all foreground IP to the partner, or bind you to their governing law. Under RA 10055 an RDI's negotiators have statutory backing to push back — but only if someone on the project reads the draft. That someone, on a small project, is you.

5.7The IPOPHL Memorandum Circulars: Reading the Operational Layer

Your syllabus assigns five IPOPHL memorandum circulars — Nos. 2020-023, 2020-024, 2020-025, 2022-016, and 2023-002 — and their purpose in this course is double: the specific procedures they carry, and the skill of reading circulars at all.

Place them first in the hierarchy you built in Chapter 3 (Figure 3.1): memorandum circulars are administrative issuances — the layer below the IP Code and its IRRs, where the office translates statute into current, operational procedure: filing modalities and electronic services, fee schedules and reductions, examination and registration guidelines, and program mechanics for the office's services. This is the layer that changes most often — circulars amend, supersede, and consolidate one another as practice evolves — which is why professionals cite them by number and date, and why the authoritative text is always the current one posted by IPOPHL, not a summary. Read each assigned circular with the Chapter 3 method: what does it operationalize (which statute or rule above it)? whom does it bind? what procedure, fee, or deadline does it change? — and then ask the Chapter 5 question: which step of my disclosure-to-filing workflow does this circular govern?

The deeper lesson is one you have now met three times: statutes give principles, IRRs give structure, and the circular layer gives the procedure you will actually follow on filing day — the form, the portal, the fee, the deadline. A research manager who has never read a circular has never actually filed anything; the five in your reading list are your supervised first ascent of the operational layer.

Key Concept
Currency check before citing: circulars supersede one another. The professional habit — verify on the IPOPHL memorandum circulars page that the number you cite is still in force — is thirty seconds that distinguishes a practitioner from a note-taker.
For the Proposal
Your budget meets this layer directly: IPOPHL fees (with reductions for small entities and schools, per the current fee circulars) are legitimate line items in an R&D budget that promises protected outputs. A proposal that budgets zero pesos for the protection it promises is telling the panel it has not read the operational layer.

Question 18 · Applying
